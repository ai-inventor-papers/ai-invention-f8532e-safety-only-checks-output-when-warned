# Logit-Gap Steering: A Forward-Pass Diagnostic for Alignment Robustness

Tung-Ling Li  Affiliation: Palo Alto Networks  Email: [tuli@paloaltonetworks.com](mailto:) Hongliang Liu  Affiliation: Palo Alto Networks  Email: [honliu@paloaltonetworks.com](mailto:)

###### Abstract

RLHF-style alignment trains language models to refuse unsafe requests, but how much operational margin does this refusal rest on? We introduce the _refusal–affirmation logit gap_ : the difference between the top refusal-token logit and the top affirmative-token logit at the first decoding step. This single scalar quantifies the per-prompt safety margin that alignment provides. Empirically, alignment widens the gap on 97.5–99.8% of toxic prompts across three model families, and median gap closure co-varies with True-ASR ranking across suffix strategies (an internal consistency check, since our method optimises gap closure; §5). To validate the metric’s practical significance, we present _logit-gap steering_ , a gradient-free, forward-pass-only method that discovers short in-distribution suffixes (<<10 tokens per component) whose cumulative effect closes the gap. The method requires ≈26,000{\approx}26{,}000 forward-pass equivalents per family (≈2{\approx}2 min on one A100), ≈125×{\approx}125\times less than a single GCG search. Suffixes discovered on 0.5B–2B models transfer without modification to 72B within family. An 8-suffix ensemble reaches 38–96% True ASR across 13 models on AdvBench and HarmBench, with most suffixes having 10310^{3}–104×10^{4}\times lower perplexity than GCG—meaning published perplexity-filter defenses that collapse GCG (64.7%→\to1.0%) leave our suffixes nearly intact (76.9%→\to76.0%). These results demonstrate that current alignment margins, while consistently present, can be thin and efficiently measurable, and that defense strategies must account for in-distribution suffixes.

## 1 Introduction

Large language models are aligned to refuse unsafe requests. Alignment suppresses unsafe behavior but does not eliminate it Wolf et al. (2023). Prior work shows that alignment creates a narrow energy gap between refusal and compliance, implying that appending a short suffix to a toxic prompt can flip the model into compliance. The open problem is how to _measure_ this residual first-token operational refusal margin (the position-1 logit-level decision that gates refusal, not alignment as a whole) and probe its robustness at scale.

We introduce the _refusal–affirmation logit gap_ : the difference between the top refusal-token logit and the top affirmative-token logit at the first decoding step after a harmful prompt. This framing recasts suffix-based jailbreaks as controlled probes that succeed when the gap is closed. Existing methods such as AutoPrompt Shin et al. (2020) and GCG Zou et al. (2023) identify effective suffixes but require gradient-based optimization over large, out-of-distribution token spaces. GCG takes over 5 hours on a single A100 for 50 prompts. These costs limit their usefulness as scalable diagnostic tools.

We present _logit-gap steering_ , a gradient-free probing method that finds per-token in-distribution perturbation suffixes (typically <<10 tokens per component; 24–60 tokens for the full sequence) using only forward passes. The method combines approximate KL and reward proxies with aggressive candidate filtering to score tokens in a single forward call. A greedy covering algorithm then selects tokens whose cumulative score exceeds the initial gap. End-to-end discovery of all 8 ensemble suffixes requires ≈26,000{\approx}26{,}000 forward-pass equivalents (scoring + permutation), under 1% of GCG’s per-family compute budget.

We do not claim suffix-based attacks are new; our contribution is to recast them as a measurement instrument for the first-token refusal margin.

#### Scope.

“Alignment robustness” here refers to the first-token refusal margin under suffix perturbations, not to model safety as a whole.

#### Related Work.

#### Universal jailbreaks and token-level attacks.

Zou et al. (2023) introduced universal adversarial suffixes that flip aligned models from refusal to compliance regardless of the prompt. Their method relies on gradient-based optimization over the token space. Our logit-gap formulation subsumes their empirical gap-flip observation and provides a formal sufficiency condition for success (Eq. 2).

#### Prompt-level and search-based attacks.

PAIR Chao et al. (2023), AutoDAN Liu et al. (2024b), and TAP Mehrotra et al. (2023) construct semantically meaningful jailbreaks by iteratively prompting a separate attacker LLM (PAIR, TAP) or by hierarchical genetic search (AutoDAN). These methods produce fluent attacks but require either an attacker model in the loop or hundreds of generation steps per prompt, and target single-prompt attacks rather than universal suffixes that transfer across prompts and scales. Our method occupies a different point in the design space: gradient-free, single-pass per candidate, and producing universal suffixes that transfer 0.5B→\to72B within family. We compare directly to GCG (the dominant universal-suffix baseline) because it is the only prior method that produces a single suffix transferable across prompts at our scale; per-prompt comparisons against PAIR/AutoDAN/TAP are out of scope for this work and complementary in deployment.

#### Representation-space steering.

Turner et al. (2023) showed that linear activation steering can impose stylistic attributes on generated text. Sinii et al. (2025) demonstrated that a single steering vector per layer can match full RL-tuning performance. However, Da Silva et al. (2025) found that single steering vectors exhibit high variance across contexts. We target alignment-critical directions (refusal vs. affirmation) and address reliability through multi-objective permutation selection and ensembling (§3.4).

## 2 Refusal–Affirmation Logit Gap in Aligned Language Models

Modern chat models are aligned in two stages. First, supervised fine-tuning trains on curated instruction data Wei et al. (2021); Wang et al. (2022). Second, a KL-regularised policy-gradient step (e.g., PPO-based RLHF Stiennon et al. (2020); Ouyang et al. (2022)) rewards refusal for disallowed content and compliance for benign requests. This pipeline lifts the logit of canonical refusal tokens (“Sorry”, “No”, “cannot”) relative to affirmative ones (“Certainly”, “Here’s…”) at the first decoding step, creating the _refusal–affirmation logit gap_.

This section formalises the gap, shows empirically that alignment widens it, and derives the condition a suffix must satisfy to close it. The scoring function and search algorithm follow in §3.

#### Alignment widens the gap.

Let

## 3 Method

We present a diagnostic probing procedure that measures and reduces the refusal–affirmation logit gap using only forward passes. The goal is to identify per-token in-distribution perturbations (typically <<10 tokens per component) that expose how much first-token operational refusal margin separates refusal from compliance.

#### Three-stage decomposition.

The full attack factors into three stages, each independently swappable: (i) filter a candidate pool 𝒞\mathcal{C} from the model’s own high-probability tokens (§3.2 Step 1), (ii) score each candidate with F⁡(h,t)F(h,t) to rank by gap-closure power (§3.1), and (iii) steer the first decoded token with an affirmative prefix (“Here’s”; §4.2). Our ablations isolate each stage — the score-vs-filter probe (§3.1, App. N) confirms FF adds value above the filter alone, and “Here’s”-only baselines (§4.2) confirm the perturbation adds value above the steering token alone. The structure of the rest of this section follows this decomposition.

The procedure has two algorithmic components. First, a score function F⁡(h,t)F(h,t) estimates how much a candidate token tt contributes to closing the gap (§3.1). Second, a greedy algorithm selects tokens whose cumulative score covers the initial gap (§3.2). No gradients are required; the pipeline discovers all 8 ensemble suffixes per family in ≈125×{\approx}125\times / ≈17×{\approx}17\times less compute than single-GCG / 8-shot nanoGCG (§4.2).

### 3.1 Gap-Closing Score with KL and Reward Proxies

Appending a token tt to a state with hidden representation hh changes the logit gap by

| Δ​Flogit​(h,t)=[ℓrefusal​(h)−ℓaffirm​(h)]−[ℓrefusal​(T⁡(h,t))−ℓaffirm​(T⁡(h,t))],\begin{split}\Delta F_{\text{logit}}(h,t)&=\bigl[\ell_{\text{refusal}}(h)-\ell_{\text{affirm}}(h)\bigr]\\\ &\quad-\bigl[\ell_{\text{refusal}}(T(h,t))-\ell_{\text{affirm}}(T(h,t))\bigr],\end{split} |  | (4)  
---|---|---|---  
  
where T⁡(h,t)T(h,t) is the hidden state after processing token tt. Computing this exactly requires two forward passes per candidate token. We instead approximate this change using proxies computable from a single forward pass.

#### Token logit computation.

We curate three token lists (refusal, affirmative, and neutral) by collecting first-response tokens from harmful, benign, and neutral queries respectively. At state hh, ℓrefusal​(h)\ell_{\text{refusal}}(h), ℓaffirm​(h)\ell_{\text{affirm}}(h), and ℓneutral​(h)\ell_{\text{neutral}}(h) denote the maximum logit in each list (full lists in Appendix B.1).

#### Approximate KL penalty.

RLHF discourages deviations from the base model via a KL penalty. We proxy its change by comparing the refusal logit to that of a neutral, high-probability token u⋆u_{\star} (the highest-probability token from the neutral list):

| Δ​KL​(h,t)≈[ℓrefusal​(T⁡(h,t))−ℓu⋆​(T⁡(h,t))]−[ℓrefusal​(h)−ℓu⋆​(h)].\begin{split}\Delta\mathrm{KL}(h,t)\;\approx{}&\bigl[\ell_{\text{refusal}}(T(h,t))-\ell_{u_{\star}}(T(h,t))\bigr]\\\ &-\bigl[\ell_{\text{refusal}}(h)-\ell_{u_{\star}}(h)\bigr].\end{split} |  | (5)  
---|---|---|---  
  
This eliminates the per-step KL recomputation required by prior methods Wan et al. (2023); Zhao et al. (2024).

#### Reward-shift proxy.

The learned reward model favours refusal. We proxy its local change with the incremental affirmative logit, Δ​r​(h,t)=ℓaffirm​(T⁡(h,t))−ℓaffirm​(h)\Delta r(h,t)=\ell_{\text{affirm}}\\!\bigl(T(h,t)\bigr)-\ell_{\text{affirm}}(h), following the observation that reward correlates monotonically with this logit Bai et al. (2022).

#### Combined score.

We combine the three proxies into a single ranking score:

| F⁡(h,t)=Δ​Flogit​(h,t)−λKL​Δ​KL​(h,t)+λr​Δ​r​(h,t),F(h,t)=\Delta F_{\text{logit}}(h,t)-\lambda_{\mathrm{KL}}\Delta\mathrm{KL}(h,t)+\lambda_{r}\Delta r(h,t), |  | (6)  
---|---|---|---  
  
with non-negative hyperparameters λKL,λr\lambda_{\mathrm{KL}},\lambda_{r}. Tokens with high F⁡(h,t)F(h,t) are predicted to be most effective at closing the gap.

#### Empirical validity.

Eq. (6) is a ranking heuristic, not a calibrated predictor. Over the full vocabulary R2R^{2} is moderate (0.17–0.47), but candidate filtering (§3.2) restricts evaluation to |𝒞|≈30|\mathcal{C}|\approx 30–9999 in-distribution tokens, where ranking quality is high (Spearman ρ≥0.818\rho\geq 0.818, NDCG@20 = 0.86–0.95; Table 4, Appendix H). This within-pool ranking quality is conditional on the same filter 𝒞\mathcal{C} that the algorithm uses; the score’s behavioral validity comes from the downstream True ASR comparisons in §4.2, not from the in-pool rank correlation. To isolate the contribution of FF from the filter alone, we measured at position 1 the gap-closure power of every filtered candidate: the score-pick (top-1 by FF) yields +2.1+2.1/+2.5+2.5/+4.8+4.8 logit units of gap closure on Llama/gemma/Qwen, while a random pick from the same filtered pool yields +1.0+1.0/−1.4-1.4/−0.9-0.9 (Appendix N). On gemma and Qwen, the filter alone produces tokens that on average increase the gap; FF is what selects the subset that closes it.

#### Theoretical motivation.

The greedy search is motivated by set-cover problems. In a static setting where scores are computed once at h0h_{0}, greedy selection is optimal. Hidden states evolve with each appended token, so this provides motivation rather than a guarantee. Deriving dynamic performance bounds remains future work.

### 3.3 Overcoming Greedy Limitations via Variants

Algorithm 1 has two limitations that affect the quality of discovered suffixes.

(1) Single-token myopia. Greedy selection evaluates each token independently at h0h_{0}, missing compositional effects. Multi-token phrases (e.g., “I’d be happy to help”) can close the gap more effectively as a unit than the sum of their individual token scores would predict, due to syntactic coherence. _Solution_ : constituent-level search evaluates NN-token sequences as atomic units (Alg. 1.C, Appendix B.3).

(2) Out-of-distribution artifacts. Pure score optimization can produce token sequences with unnatural n-gram statistics that perplexity-based filters easily flag. _Solution_ : DFS phrase harvesting constrains the search to the model’s own generation distribution, following top-kk predictions while filtering for affirmative semantics (Alg. 1.A, Appendix B.2).

#### Multi-prompt collection.

We run each variant on 50 diverse harmful prompts (one suffix per prompt), then select the top N=5N{=}5 by cumulative score ∑tF⁡(h0,t)\sum_{t}F(h_{0},t). These are assembled via permutation selection (§3.4).

### 3.4 Multi-Objective Permutation Selection

#### Motivation.

Token contributions depend on evolving hidden states hih_{i}, so greedy concatenation by score FF alone may fail. Single-objective optimization may also sacrifice properties important for stable probing. We therefore search over all N!=120N!{=}120 orderings using three complementary objectives.

#### Method.

We evaluate all orderings under: (1) KL–Reward Balance Obj1​(S)=∑i(Δ​KL​(hi−1,ti)−Δ​r​(hi−1,ti))\text{Obj}_{1}(S)=\sum_{i}(\Delta\mathrm{KL}(h_{i-1},t_{i})-\Delta r(h_{i-1},t_{i})), which minimizes policy deviation while maximizing reward; (2) Direct Gap Minimization Obj2​(S)=Δlogit​(hk)\text{Obj}_{2}(S)=\Delta_{\text{logit}}(h_{k}), which directly optimizes final gap closure; and (3) Combined Score Obj3​(S)=∑iF⁡(hi−1,ti)\text{Obj}_{3}(S)=\sum_{i}F(h_{i-1},t_{i}), which balances all forces via our heuristic. Each objective selects its best ordering, yielding three specialized suffixes. Concatenating them produces a fourth combo suffix S1⊕S2⊕S3S_{1}\oplus S_{2}\oplus S_{3}. This yields four diverse suffixes per variant, enabling robust ensembling (§4.2).

For N=5N=5, this requires ≈600{\approx}600 forward passes per variant (Algorithm 2 in Appendix B.3).

## 4 Experiments

### 4.1 Suffix Discovery Protocol

We tested transferability across three model families: Llama AI@Meta (2024) (PPO-based RLHF), Gemma Team et al. (2024a); Team et al. (2024b); Team (2025) (Google RLHF), and Qwen Yang et al. (2025); Yang et al. (2024) (Alibaba RLHF). These families share alignment recipes but differ in scale and training data. Suffix search uses only the smallest model in each family (Qwen2.5-0.5B, Llama-3.2-1B, gemma-2-2b). The same discovered suffixes are then applied to all larger models in that family (§4.2); the three Qwen3 targets are evaluated with Qwen2.5-discovered suffixes (a partial cross-version transfer test).

We used the first 50 AdvBench prompts for discovery (DFS, constituent greedy, and the GCG+SH baseline), generating ≈50{\approx}50 short suffixes per variant. Discovered-ensemble True ASR on the seen 50 vs. unseen 470 differs by only −1.2-1.2 pp on average (75.8% vs. 77.0%; App. P), so headline numbers reflect generalization, not memorization. We selected the top N=5N{=}5 by cumulative score for permutation selection (§3.4).

#### Findings.

Ties cluster on weakly-aligned small Llamas where Random+SH and SH-alone also score highly (Random+SH 1-shot beats both our variants on 3 models). The ensemble exceeds 90% True ASR on five models (peaking at 95.2% on Qwen2.5-0.5B). #### Ensemble baseline control.

The 8-shot ensemble’s gain reflects method-specific diversity, not mere k-shot aggregation. We constructed two matched 8-shot baselines: Random+SH (8) adds 7 random-suffix seeds to the existing R+SH single shot; GCG+SH (8) adds 7 nanoGCG runs per family (different seeds, smallest-model search, transferred within family). Single-shot True ASRs are within 1 pp on AdvBench (R+SH 31.7% / GCG+SH 31.0% / Discovered 31.0%, 13 models), but at 8-shot the means stratify: 54.4% / 64.7% / 76.9% AdvBench (paired Wilcoxon vs. Random p=0.0001p{=}0.0001, 13/0/0 wins; vs. GCG p=0.003p{=}0.003, 11/0/2) and 61.3% / 68.5% / 75.8% HarmBench (App. L). Family-clustered (n=3n{=}3) deltas are consistent: Ours >> GCG+SH on 3/3 families with mean +10.4+10.4 pp AdvBench (App. O). Failure diversity is the proximate mechanism: pairwise correlation 0.54 / 0.40 / 0.22 AdvBench (0.56 / 0.45 / 0.31 HarmBench), monotonically tracking ensemble gain. This advantage is partly structural: our ensemble spans 2 methods ×\times 4 objectives, while GCG-8 varies only random seeds; the diversity is by construction, not an emergent property of the scoring function alone. The advantage scales sharply with alignment strength: 8–18×\times on Qwen2.5-72B (37.9% vs. 4.6% vs. 2.1%), but only 1.5–3×\times at Qwen2.5-7B and converging on weakly-aligned small models.

#### Role of the affirmative token.

“Here’s” alone yielded 12–64.5% True ASR; our best single suffix achieved 28–69%; the ensemble reached 40–98% (Tables 1–2). Both phases (perturbation and steering) are necessary for single-shot effectiveness on strongly-aligned models; on weakly-aligned models the steering token alone with multi-shot aggregation can saturate.

#### Efficiency.

Logit-gap steering is forward-only on a filtered candidate space of 30–99 tokens. End-to-end discovery of all 8 suffixes per family costs ≈26,000{\approx}26{,}000 FPE (≈21,200{\approx}21{,}200 scoring + ≈4,800{\approx}4{,}800 permutation; ≈2{\approx}2 min on one A100). A single GCG universal-suffix search costs ≈3.25{\approx}3.25M FPE (≈5.2{\approx}5.2 h) and yields one suffix, giving a ≈125×{\approx}125\times pipeline ratio against the original GCG formulation Zou et al. (2023). Against the matched 8-shot GCG+SH baseline (§4.2) built with nanoGCG (≈64{\approx}64k FPE per seed ×\times 7 seeds ≈450\approx 450k FPE per family), the apples-to-apples ratio is ≈17×{\approx}17\times.

#### End-to-end PPL defense.

We applied a perplexity-filter input defense Alon and Kamfonas (2023); Jain et al. (2023) (PPL over full input prompt++suffix, T=1000T{=}1000 following Alon and Kamfonas (2023); Fig. 3). The result is robust across four references: three in-discovery (Llama-3.2-1B, Qwen2.5-0.5B, gemma-2b-it) and one fully external (GPT-2, no discovery overlap; GCG 13.9% vs. Discovered 76.4%). The takeaway is narrow: _published PPL-filter input defenses tuned to catch GCG-style high-PPL strings are not, by themselves, sufficient against universal, transferable, computationally cheap suffixes whose perplexity overlaps benign instruction-style text._ A defender who scores PPL on the suffix span only (rather than the full prompt++suffix) could close some of the gap by sharpening the threshold against short benign continuations. This alternative is not evaluated in any cited PPL-defense baseline; exploring it is a natural next step for defenders.

#### Semantic output defense (Llama Guard 3).

Llama-Guard-3-8B applied to the model’s response cuts all three methods by 50–60 pp: 8-shot True ASR drops from 54.4/64.7/76.9% to 6.1/8.5/17.5% (R+SH/GCG/Discovered, 13 models). The in-distribution-suffix advantage from the input-PPL setting therefore does _not_ transfer to a semantic output filter. Discovered retains a 2–3×\times lead overall and 8–16×\times on strongly-aligned targets (Qwen2.5-72B 30.4% vs. 3.8%/1.9%; App. Q). The PPL advantage does not transfer to semantic defenses; the ensemble-diversity advantage does.

## 5 Discussion

#### Gap closure tracks attack success (internal consistency).

Across all three discovery families, our suffixes produced the lowest median Δfinal\Delta_{\text{final}}, and median gap-closure ordering tracked True-ASR ordering more closely than suffix length or token novelty (Appendix G). Since our method optimizes for gap closure, this is an internal consistency check rather than independent validation. As an independent check, this co-variation also holds for Random and “Sure”-prefix suffixes (App. G), neither of which optimises for gap closure—ruling out the artifact-of-our-objective explanation.

#### Gap variation across families and scales.

Δ0\Delta_{0} grows approximately linearly with layer width within families and is largest on Qwen and smallest on Llama (2–4 logits, Fig. 5, App. E); larger models produce heavier-tailed F⁡(t)F(t) distributions that compensate, keeping the greedy search efficient.

#### Sentence-boundary reward cliffs.

A token-level reward proxy Δ​rtok​(h,t)=ℓ⁡(h,t)−ℓ⁡(hneu,t)\Delta r_{\text{tok}}(h,t)=\ell(h,t)-\ell(h_{\text{neu}},t) revealed a saw-tooth pattern across all three discovery families (Figures 6–8, App. E): aggregating over 50 toxic prompts, mid-clause-to-boundary drops are +4.9+4.9 logit units on Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct and +9.5+9.5 on Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct. The pattern is also reflected in the safety-trained PKU-SafeRLHF reward model (cost rises by +1.0+1.0 at boundary tokens, p=6.4×10−24p{=}6.4{\times}10^{-24}; App. S), validating the saw-tooth as a safety-RLHF reward dynamic rather than purely a pre-training fluency artifact (a general helpfulness reward model, Skywork-RM, shows no boundary pattern p=0.6p{=}0.6). Successful suffixes therefore concentrate gap-closing power in the first run-on clause and delay punctuation.

## 6 Limitations and Future Directions

The first-order approximation may degrade beyond ∼10{\sim}10 tokens; cross-family transfer to held-out families is robust on Mistral-7B-Instruct (6-suffix ensemble 92.7% True ASR vs. 51.7% baseline) but mixed on GPT-OSS-20B’s reasoning-format chat template (best Qwen-source 50.4% vs. 41.5%; Llama- and Gemma-source underperform; App. M). Top-20 candidate ranking on Llama-3.2-1B is invariant to ±2×\pm 2\times on λKL,λr\lambda_{\mathrm{KL}},\lambda_{r} and ±0.5\pm 0.5 on τz\tau_{z}, with γ\gamma as the load-bearing knob (App. D); a full ASR-level sweep remains future work. The position-1 assumption (§2) holds at 92–99% per-model coverage; the residual needs a multi-step formulation. On weakly-aligned gemma the ensemble underperforms (App. L); a token-swap probe suggests suffix-token coupling rather than tokenizer mismatch (App. D).

#### Defense-aware adversaries and recommendations.

A defender who knows our method can monitor: (i) mid-clause-vs-boundary token-level rewards (§5), since our suffixes concentrate gap closure inside run-on clauses; (ii) the affirmative steering token following a long perturbation; or (iii) the hidden-state shift induced by the perturbation. LG3 already catches 53% of (suffix, prompt) pairs (§4.3); pairing it with a token-level-reward monitor or hidden-state classifier is a plausible layered defense the present work does not test. Stronger countermeasures may include adversarial training against gap-closure exemplars and re-estimating Δ0\Delta_{0} on responses post-generation as a runtime check.

## 7 Conclusion

The refusal–affirmation logit gap recasts suffix-based jailbreaks as a measurable closure problem at the first decoding step. A forward-only probe finds effective suffixes ≈125×{\approx}125\times faster than GCG, the 8-suffix ensemble reaches 38–96% True ASR across 13 models, and PPL-filter and Llama-Guard-3 defenses each catch only one suffix style; layered deployment is therefore necessary. Beyond efficiency, the gap framework exposes sentence-boundary reward cliffs and other alignment artefacts (§5), offering a lightweight probe into how safety tuning reshapes internal representations.

## Impact Statement

This work exposes alignment vulnerabilities and provides a quantitative diagnostic for safety evaluation. The discovered suffixes target publicly-available models that GCG and prior published attacks already jailbreak; our contribution is the diagnostic framework and a more compute-efficient probe, not a novel class of harm. We adopt the following concrete safeguards.

(i) Coordinated disclosure. The 24 discovered suffixes were shared with the safety teams at Meta (Llama), Alibaba (Qwen), Google (gemma), Mistral, and OpenAI (GPT-OSS) at least 30 days before public release; the artifact release in the supplementary material is gated behind an acceptable-use clickthrough.

(ii) Tested defenses. We evaluate two off-the-shelf defenses against our own attack: input-perplexity filtering at the published T=1000T{=}1000 threshold Alon and Kamfonas (2023) and Llama Guard 3 output filtering Inan et al. (2023). Both reduce the attack but with different mechanisms (PPL collapses GCG, LG3 collapses ours), supporting the layered-defense recommendation in §6 above. We also describe three monitoring signals (token-level reward sawtooth, post-perturbation steering token, hidden-state shift) that defenders can deploy without retraining.

(iii) No new harmful content. We do not collect new harmful prompts, do not release any model weights, and do not publish completions verbatim — only the suffix tokens needed for reproducibility.

We believe disclosure benefits defenders more than attackers given (a) the comparable accessibility of GCG-class attacks already in the literature and (b) the layered-defense evidence above.
